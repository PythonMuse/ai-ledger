# AI in Accounting Is Not the Wild West Anymore

*But It's Also Not Plug-and-Play*

---

**By Svetlana Toohey**
*Originally published: March 2026 | Last updated: November 2026*

> **November 2026 update:** This article has been refreshed to reflect additional AI governance and audit guidance issued since its original publication in March 2026.

![AI in Accounting Is Not the Wild West Anymore](./visuals/04_visual_front.png)

For the past year, many finance teams have been quietly experimenting with AI.

It usually starts with harmless questions:

- "Can you summarize this contract?"
- "Can you categorize these transactions?"
- "Can you draft variance commentary for the monthly report?"

Then someone asks the question that makes the entire room slightly uncomfortable:

"If AI helped produce this... will the auditors accept it?"

Fair question.

Accounting is not known for its enthusiasm toward mysterious black boxes.

The good news is that the profession is beginning to build real guardrails around AI use in business processes.

The less exciting news is that there is still no official rulebook titled:

*How to Let ChatGPT Touch Your Close Without Giving Your Auditor Heartburn.*

We are currently in a transition phase where regulators, accounting firms, and professional organizations are converging around the same principle:

**AI can be used responsibly in accounting -- but it must operate inside existing governance and control frameworks.**

Let's unpack what that actually means.

---

## What the Accounting Rulebook Actually Says (So Far)

One of the biggest misconceptions right now is that regulators have already issued detailed rules governing AI-generated accounting outputs.

They haven't.

As of November 2026, we have not found a FASB (Financial Accounting Standards Board) Accounting Standards Update that specifically addresses AI-generated financial information.

The closest it has come is the recent update to internal-use software accounting (ASU 2025-06). The Board considered questions about AI training costs and decided not to address them in that targeted update.

> **Go deeper: what the FASB actually said.** The Basis for Conclusions paragraphs and what they do (and do not) mean are in [the technical appendix](./technical-appendix.md#1-the-fasb-and-asu-2025-06).

In other words:

**The standards have not yet caught up to the technology.**

But that does not mean AI sits outside the accounting rulebook.

GAAP applies to the financial statements whether a number was produced by a person, a spreadsheet, or a large language model. Management is still responsible for the financial reporting and for the internal controls behind it. Auditing standards govern the auditors, not the company.

Instead of writing new rules, standard setters and professional bodies are applying existing principles to new technology.

Four frameworks currently shape how AI should be used in accounting environments:

- **Accounting standards** (FASB)
- **Audit standards** (PCAOB / AICPA)
- **Internal control frameworks** (COSO)
- **Professional ethics** (the [AICPA Code of Professional Conduct](https://pub.aicpa.org/codeofconduct/Ethics.aspx), for CPAs)

That last one is easy to forget. The Code's principles of integrity, objectivity and due care, together with its rules on competence and confidentiality, do not pause because a co-pilot drafted the first version. If you paste client data into an unapproved AI tool, that is a confidentiality question before it is a technology question.

Together, these provide the guardrails.

## Technology-Assisted Auditing

The PCAOB (Public Company Accounting Oversight Board, which sets auditing standards for audits of public companies) amended its audit evidence standards to clarify auditors' responsibilities when they use technology-assisted analysis of electronic information.

The amendments are effective for audits of financial statements for fiscal years beginning on or after December 15, 2025.

What does this mean in practice?

Technology can help auditors analyze entire data populations when that is appropriate.

However, these amendments do not require full-population testing or eliminate traditional audit sampling. Auditors must still design procedures appropriate to the assessed risks and obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence.

They are not generative AI standards either. And they do not mean auditors suddenly trust AI outputs automatically.

What they do sharpen is attention to the *information* going in. A spreadsheet or a screenshot is not automatically bad evidence, and a polished data extract is not automatically good evidence. What matters is whether it is reliable and fit for the purpose.

> **Go deeper: the amendments themselves.** Which standards changed, what the PCAOB said about sampling and AI, and how auditors evaluate the reliability of company-provided data are in [the technical appendix](./technical-appendix.md#2-the-pcaob-technology-assisted-analysis-amendments).

Which brings us to one of the most important developments.

## COSO's New Guidance for Generative AI

On February 23, 2026, COSO (the Committee of Sponsoring Organizations of the Treadway Commission, the body behind the internal control framework most US companies use for SOX) released new guidance called:

**"Achieving Effective Internal Control Over Generative AI (GenAI)."**

You can explore it here:
[https://www.coso.org/generative-ai](https://www.coso.org/generative-ai)

This publication is one of the most practical resources currently available for organizations integrating AI into business workflows.

To be clear about what it is: practical guidance that builds on the 2013 Internal Control -- Integrated Framework. It is not a new mandatory regulation or a standalone compliance standard.

Instead of creating a brand-new governance model, COSO did something refreshingly practical.

It showed how Generative AI risks can be governed using the existing COSO Internal Control -- Integrated Framework.

That means applying the same five familiar components already used for financial reporting and SOX compliance:

**Control Environment**
Define ownership, governance policies, and oversight of AI systems.

**Risk Assessment**
Identify risks such as prompt manipulation, model drift, opaque reasoning, and data leakage.

**Control Activities**
Implement approval workflows, validation checks, and access controls.

**Information & Communication**
Ensure traceability of AI inputs, outputs, and decisions.

**Monitoring Activities**
Continuously evaluate performance and detect unexpected behavior.

In other words:

**AI does not replace internal control. It becomes another system operating inside it.**

For finance teams, this is extremely important.

It means AI workflows designed inside a structured control environment are far better positioned when the auditor comes asking.

## What the AICPA Is Now Saying About AI

Since this article first appeared, the AICPA (the American Institute of Certified Public Accountants, the national professional body for CPAs) has published several practical resources on AI.

None of them is a new auditing standard. All of them point in the same direction.

| When it came out | What the AICPA published | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| April 29, 2026 | [Small Firm Generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) Policy Template](https://on-demand.aicpalearningcenter.org/resources/download/aicpa-small-firm-generative-artificial-intelligence-ai-policy-template) | A practical starting point for small accounting firms writing an AI use policy for publicly available large language models. It is a template, so adapt it to your firm's own risks and circumstances. |
| September 11, 2026 | [TQA Section 9561: Effect of the Service Organization's Use of AI on SOC 1 and SOC 2 Examinations](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/resources/download/tqa-section-9561-soc-examinations-effect-of-the-service-organizations-use-of-ai-on-soc1-and-soc2-examinations) | Technical questions and answers on how a service organization's use of AI is considered within the existing SOC examination frameworks. Relevant to service organizations, their examiners, and finance teams that rely on SOC reports from vendors. |
| October 2026 (resource dated October 12; announced October 13) | [Artificial Intelligence (AI) Considerations for Financial Statement Auditors](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/resources/article/explore-new-aicpa-guidance-on-artificial-intelligence-ai-and-the-financial-statement-audit) | A nonauthoritative practice aid covering both a company's use of AI in financial reporting or internal controls and the auditor's own use of AI in performing the audit. |

The October practice aid deserves the most attention.

It looks at AI from both sides of the audit: how a company's use of AI affects the audit, and how audit firms use AI themselves. Across both, it reinforces familiar ideas: risk assessment, reliable data, quality management, appropriate supervision and review, professional skepticism and professional judgment.

None of those ideas is new. That is exactly the point. The practice aid does not create new requirements; it shows how the responsibilities auditors already have apply when AI is in the picture.

> **Go deeper: inside the practice aid.** Both sides of the practice aid, with the AICPA's own words, are in [the technical appendix](./technical-appendix.md#3-the-aicpa-practice-aid-and-the-soc-2-proposal).

**Why should accounting professionals care about guidance written for auditors?**

Because it is a preview of the questions you are likely to be asked. If your team uses AI anywhere in the close, reconciliations or reporting, your auditor may want to understand:

- where AI is used and what it touches
- what data it relies on, and how you know that data is reliable
- who reviews its outputs, and what evidence of that review exists
- what controls surround it

An accounting team that can answer those questions before fieldwork starts is in a much better position than one hearing them for the first time in January.

One more development to watch: in October 2026, the AICPA [proposed an update to the SOC 2 Trust Services Criteria](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/news/article/aicpa-proposes-update-of-soc-2-r-trust-services-criteria-to-enhance-the-relevance-usability-and-value-of-cpas-reports) that adds considerations for AI. **It is a proposal, not an effective requirement**, and the final version may change.

The pattern across all of this is consistent:

**The technology is changing. Our professional responsibilities are not.**

## What the Big Four Are Saying

While regulators move cautiously, the Big Four accounting firms have already published guidance on how AI should be adopted within finance functions.

Across Deloitte, PwC, EY, and KPMG, the recommendations point in the same direction, even if each firm emphasizes different pieces.

Taken together, they suggest corporate finance teams should:

- maintain an inventory of AI tools used in finance
- classify how each tool interacts with financial data
- ensure human oversight of accounting conclusions
- document inputs and outputs
- integrate AI workflows into internal control over financial reporting

Since March, several newer publications have added detail:

- **Deloitte**, [Putting new COSO GenAI internal controls guidance into practice](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/services/audit-assurance/blogs/accounting-finance/coso-ai-framework-internal-controls-for-generative-ai.html) (June 2026): governance roles, a use-case inventory, and how much human involvement each use needs.
- **KPMG**, [COSO releases roadmap on internal control over generative AI](https://kpmg.com/us/en/frv/reference-library/2026/coso-releases-roadmap-internal-control-over-generative-ai.html) (April 2026): how COSO's guidance applies to internal control over financial reporting.
- **EY**, [AI audit readiness for financial reporting](https://www.ey.com/en_us/insights/assurance/ai-audit-readiness-for-financial-reporting) (September 2026): reproducibility, data lineage, human review, and governing changes to AI systems. That last one is close to the heart of the PythonMuse approach: if the model or prompt changed, you should know about it before the auditor does.
- **PwC**, [Financial reporting oversight](https://www.pwc.com/us/en/services/governance-insights-center/library/financial-reporting-oversight.html): audit committee oversight of financial reporting, including where AI is entering the process.

One important distinction: these are professional insights and recommendations, not accounting or auditing standards. They are useful, but they are not the rulebook.

> **Go deeper: what each firm recommends.** The specific recommendations, attributed publication by publication, are in [the technical appendix](./technical-appendix.md#4-big-four-publications-in-more-detail).

Notice something interesting.

None of these recommendations say:

*"Don't use AI."*

They say:

**Use AI -- but govern it properly.**

## The Real Risk Isn't AI

The real risk is messy processes.

Let's be honest.

Many accounting workflows already depend on:

- massive Excel workbooks
- manual exports
- copy-paste transformations
- undocumented formulas
- filenames like `final_v8_THIS_ONE_FOR_REAL.xlsx`

If anything, AI adoption is forcing the profession to confront an uncomfortable truth:

**If the workflow is messy, AI will simply replicate the mess faster.**

Which brings us to the practical question.

## How Accounting Professionals Should Structure AI Workflows

The simplest principle is this:

**Every output must be traceable.**

An AI-supported accounting workflow should include four elements:

![Traceable AI Workflow](./visuals/04_traceable_workflow.png)
*Figure: Four elements of an audit-ready AI workflow.*

### 1. Defined Inputs

Source data must be identifiable and preserved.

Examples include:

- GL exports
- contract documents
- bank transaction files
- reconciliation data

Before the data goes in, check that it is complete and accurate (does the export tie to the trial balance?). Limit who can access it, and keep it in line with your data security and retention policies.

The key question: *Could someone later verify exactly what data the model saw?*

### 2. Transparent Transformation

If AI summarizes or analyzes information, the process must be understandable.

You do not need to reverse-engineer the entire neural network.

But you should know:

- what question was asked (the prompt or instructions)
- what data was provided
- which model and workflow version were used
- what actions any tools took along the way
- what output was generated
- how that output was validated

How much you keep should be proportionate to risk. A draft email summary needs less than a revenue cut-off analysis.

Two clarifications worth making. First, you are documenting what the workflow *observably did*, not trying to reconstruct the model's private internal reasoning. Second, "reproducible" does not necessarily mean an LLM produces word-for-word identical output on every run. It means someone can understand, re-perform and test what happened.

### 3. Human Review

AI can assist.

But it should not make final accounting decisions.

AI can help with:

- drafting commentary
- summarizing contracts
- categorizing transactions

Humans should still approve:

- journal entries
- accounting treatments
- financial disclosures

And keep evidence that they did. A reviewer's sign-off on significant judgments, exceptions and material outputs is part of the control.

This does not mean every low-risk automated transaction needs a human to click "approve." Apply review where the risk is, the same way you would for any other automated process.

### 4. Evidence Retention

Finally, retain enough information to answer the auditor's favorite question:

*"How do you know this number is correct?"*

If you can show:

- source data
- transformation process
- AI output
- human review

Then the result is far easier to defend, and no longer a mysterious artifact.

One honest caveat: documentation, traceability and validation *support* audit readiness, but they do not automatically become sufficient appropriate audit evidence. The auditor still decides whether the information is reliable, relevant and sufficient for their purpose. Your job is to make that assessment easy.

### Where to Start

If you are not sure where to begin, PythonMuse recommends two simple steps. This is our suggested starting point, not a regulatory mandate:

1. **An AI use policy** that says which tools are approved, for what, and with what data.
2. **An AI use case register**: an inventory of AI-assisted processes. For each one, record the financial reporting impact, the owner, the approved tools, the key risks, the controls, and what evidence you keep.

It is not glamorous. Neither is a fixed asset register, and you would not run without one of those either.

> **Templates to get you started.** The free [Accounting and Finance AI Governance repository](https://github.com/PythonMuse/accounting_and_finance-ai-governance) includes an [AI policy template](https://github.com/PythonMuse/accounting_and_finance-ai-governance/tree/main/docs/ai-policy.md) and an [approved use cases register](https://github.com/PythonMuse/accounting_and_finance-ai-governance/tree/main/docs/approved-use-cases.md). Adapt them to your organization's own risks and circumstances.

## Before Audit Season Begins

With year-end approaching, now is a good time for corporate finance teams to review where AI is being used in financial reporting and accounting processes.

Are those workflows operating within the applicable professional standards and your existing internal control framework? Can you explain and demonstrate how AI-assisted outputs were validated?

These are questions worth answering before audit season begins, rather than waiting for the auditor to ask. Auditors are much happier to find a well-documented process than to hear "the AI did it."

---

## One Final Thought

The goal of AI in accounting is not to eliminate professional judgment.

The goal is to eliminate the parts of the job that never required judgment in the first place.

Sorting transactions. Summarizing documents. Drafting explanations. Reconciling large data sets.

If AI can take those tasks off our plates, accountants can spend more time doing what actually matters:

**Understanding the business and explaining the numbers.**

And fortunately, that is still something AI cannot do nearly as well as a good accounting professional.
